Case Study  ·  Built for internal use

One dashboard.
Your entire operation.

Built for internal use. A custom AI-native operating system that runs a solo advisory and app development practice. It connects ClickUp, Gmail and Google Workspace, Slack, GitHub, Buffer, Kit, Plausible and Claude in one dashboard, and handles the repetitive parts of the work.

Situation

Running a solo advisory and app development practice means constant context-switching. Tasks live in ClickUp. Client emails arrive in Gmail. Finances are scattered across bank statements and email receipts. Content ideas sit in Slack. No off-the-shelf tool connected all of that in a way that matched how a one-person practice works.

The options were to pay for several separate tools and check them all by hand, or to build the right tool. I built it.

Objective

One place to see and run the whole practice, with the repetitive work done overnight and a person approving everything that matters. Four jobs in particular:

01

Overnight execution

Tasks assigned to Claude in ClickUp are drafted or researched by morning, ready to review.

02

Finance automation

Expense and revenue emails are parsed so entries can be logged in one click.

03

Content pipeline

An idea becomes a brief, a task and a tracked piece of content.

04

Idea to action

Ideas posted to Slack are assessed with a clear verdict: go, needs more thought, or park longer.

The advisory board and the marketing round table

Two groups of AI personas sit inside the dashboard. I give each group a question, they present their views and debate, and a secretary who never takes part in the debate writes up the decision. The record is saved to Google Drive. Each persona sees only the information relevant to its role, and each is built to challenge the idea, never the person holding it.

Advisory board

Business decisions

A CFO, a Chief Privacy Officer, a CMO, a Founder Coach, a Technical and Product lead and a Strategy lead. The Founder Coach sees overdue and stale tasks to spot avoidance patterns, and the Technical and Product lead sees the open product backlogs.

Marketing round table

Content and marketing

A Strategist, a Drafter, a Critic, a CMO, an SEO agent and an Analytics and GEO agent. Together they work a piece of content from brief to draft to critique. The SEO agent is still a placeholder until it has a live data feed.

Decisions and the reasoning

Build or buy

Build the right tool

Connecting six platforms the way a one-person practice actually works was not something I could buy, so I built it around my own workflow.

Control

A person approves everything

Every overnight result waits for review with three choices: approve, send back with notes, or take over. Nothing goes out on its own.

Reliability

Route writes through one endpoint

When the ClickUp connector proved unreliable under load, I moved all task updates to a single batch endpoint in the dashboard and kept the connector for reading only.

Results

The system has run daily since May 2026. It handles overnight first drafts and research, parses expense and revenue emails, produces a morning briefing, and runs the advisory board and the marketing round table. It has also failed in instructive ways, and most of what I learned came from those failures.

What I learned

Check the output, not the status

The content pipeline once reported 12 posts as ready. On inspection, 10 of them had no copy, only refusal messages. A status that says done tells you the process ran, not that it worked.

Automation exposes the quality of the process

I automated first drafts, and the drafts did not sound like me. I paused drafting and moved to a stories-first approach, where the ideas come from things that really happened.

A check that catches the same problem every time is a warning

In one review, 5 of 6 debate rounds were flagged for invented context. The automatic regeneration kept producing a different wrong answer. The real cause was a prompt with no instruction for what to do when information is missing.

Silent failures are the expensive ones

A three-line helper that started tasks without waiting for them was hiding failures across the pipeline. Finding it meant reading the code for the symptom, not sampling at random.

Working and usable are different bars

A feature that worked end to end still failed the simple test of whether I could find what it produced. I now ask both questions.

Verify against the live system, not the documentation

Buffer's documentation and several searches disagreed about what its API could return. Calling it settled the question in a minute.

How this connects to my client work

When I help a business adopt automation or AI, I start with the process and not the tool: where work is actually getting stuck, who approves what, and how we will know the output is good. Building and running my own system is where I test those questions first.
