More than half of Canadian businesses are using AI tools right now. Most of them don't have a policy, a plan, or a way to know if it's working. That's not a competitive advantage — it's a liability dressed up in good intentions.

Let me show you what I mean.

## What the 58% stat actually means (and what it doesn't)

NOVIPRO's 2026 IT Trends Report found that 58% of Canadian companies are already using AI solutions ([p.15](https://www.novipro.com/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}). If you're one of them, you're in good company — and that number is real; this isn't a survey of tech companies or venture-backed startups. It's Canadian businesses, across sectors, at a scale that reflects where the market actually is.

But the part of the data that doesn't make the headline: of those companies using AI, only 4% say it's central to their business model ([NOVIPRO, p.15](https://www.novipro.com/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}). And according to the same research, 42% of small businesses — those under 100 employees — are still in experimentation mode; running pilots, trying tools, seeing what sticks before committing to anything ([NOVIPRO, p.17](https://www.novipro.com/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}).

You may have seen Statistics Canada's Q2 2026 number, which [puts AI adoption at just 19.2%](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/11-621-m/11-621-m2026010-eng.htm){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}, and wondered why it looks so different from NOVIPRO's 58%. It's not a contradiction — they're measuring different things. NOVIPRO captures whether a company is using AI solutions in any capacity, including pilots and early experiments; Statistics Canada measures whether AI is being used in the actual production of goods and services. Both numbers are accurate. The gap between them — 39 percentage points separating "we use AI sometimes" from "AI is integrated into our operations" — is where most Canadian businesses actually live.

The [Canadian Federation of Independent Business](https://www.cfib-fcei.ca/en/research-economic-analysis/ai-adoption){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} has been tracking this too. Their AI adoption research shows a consistent pattern among small business owners: awareness is high, active adoption is uneven, and the businesses most likely to report confusion are the ones who adopted tools without a framework for evaluating whether they work. That's not a technology problem — it's an operations problem.

## The difference between using AI and having an AI strategy

Using AI tools isn't a strategy; it's a starting point. The distinction matters because one protects you and one doesn't.

When your team starts using AI tools without a formal approach — and I see this in organisations of every size — what you get is a patchwork: five different team members using five different tools, none of them integrated, none of them measured, and nobody quite sure what's being shared with which platform or what the vendor's data retention policy says. It works just well enough to feel like progress. It's not.

An AI strategy has four components. Not a vision statement; not a slide deck about innovation. Four concrete things:

**Policy.** What tools are approved for use? What kinds of work can be done with them? What data is never allowed to go near them? If your team can't answer those three questions without having to think about it, you don't have a policy — you have a gap.

**Governance.** Who decides which tools get adopted, and on what basis? Who's responsible if something goes wrong — a data breach, a compliance failure, an output that's factually wrong in a way that matters to a client? In most small businesses, this isn't anyone's specific job; which means it's implicitly everyone's job; which means it's actually no one's.

**Measurement.** Is the tool producing better outcomes than what it replaced? "Everyone seems happier" and "things feel faster" aren't measurements. If you can't point to a number, you don't know if it's working. (That's the subject of [the next post in this series](/blog/measure-ai-tool-effectiveness.html) — I'll go deep on the measurement question there.)

**Vendor selection.** Are you choosing tools based on what they can actually do for how your team works — or based on what the vendor is demonstrating? These are very different questions, and the demo version of an AI tool is almost always more impressive than the Tuesday-at-3pm version.

## What happens when you skip the strategy

The most common outcome isn't dramatic. Nobody loses data overnight. Nobody gets an unexpected bill. What happens is slower and harder to see: your team uses AI in ways that are inconsistent, inefficient, or quietly risky — and because nobody's measuring it, nobody notices until something goes wrong.

Let me be specific about the risk categories, because "risk" in the abstract tends to make people's eyes glaze over.

**Data exposure.** When your team uses a public AI tool — ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Claude — and pastes in customer information, financial figures, or proprietary business details, that data may be used to train the model, may be stored on servers in another country, and may be accessible to the vendor in ways your current privacy policy doesn't account for. Under PIPEDA — Canada's federal [Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} — you have specific obligations around how you collect, use, and disclose personal information, including when that information flows through third-party tools hosted outside Canada. Most small businesses haven't thought about this. Most of them should.

**Inconsistent output.** When different people on your team use different tools with different prompting approaches and different review standards, you don't get AI efficiency — you get inconsistency at AI speed. Proposals that don't match your voice. Reports that contain errors nobody caught because the format looked credible. Client-facing content that doesn't reflect your actual position.

**Wasted spend.** AI tool subscriptions add up quietly. A team of ten can easily be running $300–500 per month in individual AI subscriptions — on top of whatever enterprise licensing the organisation has added. If nobody's audited which tools are actually being used, which are duplicating functionality, and which people signed up for in a moment of enthusiasm and haven't opened since, that's not an innovation investment; it's a slow leak.

Here's the counter-argument worth holding alongside all of this: [BDC research](https://www.bdc.ca/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} finds that 97% of Canadian SMEs that have adopted AI report tangible benefits. The tools can genuinely work. The businesses that see those results, in my experience, are the ones who went into adoption with a plan — not the ones who figured it out after the fact.

## What an AI strategy actually looks like for a Canadian SMB

It doesn't have to be a 40-page document. For a company of 10 to 50 people, an AI strategy can be three pages and a spreadsheet — and it can be built in a focused work session if someone makes it their job to actually do it.

**An approved tools list.** Simple. These tools, for these purposes, for these roles. Everything not on the list requires approval before use. This doesn't need to be restrictive — it just needs to exist, so your team knows what's in bounds and what requires a conversation first.

**Data handling rules.** Clear guidance on what can and can't go into AI tools. Personally identifiable information, financial data, anything covered by a client confidentiality agreement — none of it goes into a public AI tool without a compliant enterprise agreement in place. This is both a PIPEDA compliance issue and a client trust issue; both matter.

**Named ownership.** Who's responsible for evaluating new tools, fielding team questions, and responding if something goes wrong? In a 15-person company, this might be 20% of one person's job. That's completely reasonable. What's not reasonable is leaving it undefined.

**A review cadence.** AI tools and the regulations around them are both changing fast enough that a policy you wrote six months ago may already have gaps. Commit to reviewing it quarterly — even a 30-minute scan to ask "does this still make sense?" is better than assuming it does.

## Where to start if you're behind

If you're reading this and thinking "we have none of this in place," the right response isn't to feel behind — it's to start with inventory.

Before you can build a policy, you need to know what tools are actually in use. Ask your team. Not in a way that implies there will be consequences for honest answers (there shouldn't be), but in a way that makes clear you're trying to protect them and the business. Most people will tell you the truth when they believe the question is genuine. Most of them have been waiting for someone to ask.

Once you know what's in play, you can figure out what needs a policy, what needs better governance, and what should probably go. That list won't take long to build. What it tells you might surprise you.

The businesses I see doing AI well aren't the ones who got there first — they're the ones who took enough time, early enough, to ask "what are we actually doing here, and does it make sense?" You can still be one of them.
