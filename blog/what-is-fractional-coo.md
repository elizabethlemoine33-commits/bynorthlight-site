A fractional COO is a senior operator who joins your executive team part-time and owns outcomes — not a consultant who hands you a deck and leaves. That's the whole answer. The rest of this post is for founders trying to figure out whether they actually need one.

## What is a fractional COO and how is it different from consulting?

<div class="answer-block"><p>A fractional COO is an experienced operations executive who works part-time (typically 10–20 hours per week), integrates into your leadership team, and takes ownership of operational outcomes — not a consultant who takes notes and sends you a deck.</p></div>

That ownership piece is what separates the role from consulting. A consultant diagnoses, recommends, and exits. A fractional COO stays, executes, and gets held accountable when something doesn't work. They're in your weekly leadership meetings. They're making the call on whether to hire for that open role or reorganise around it. They're the person your team leads bring their operational problems to, and they're responsible for the solution landing well — not just for having documented the right answer.

The "fractional" part means they're not working 40 hours a week. Most fractional COO engagements run 10–20 hours per week — enough to be integrated into your operations and leadership meetings, but not full-time. They're in your business enough to know what's actually happening and own the outcomes.

This isn't an interim role — someone parachuting in to steady the ship while you hire the real COO. It's a permanent part-time executive model designed for businesses that need senior operational leadership but don't yet need (or can't yet afford) a full-time hire.

## What a fractional COO does week-to-week

The abstract definition is fine, but it doesn't tell you what's actually happening on a Tuesday. Here's what a fractional COO's week usually looks like — not as a job description, but as the actual work:

They're in your weekly leadership meeting. Not as an observer — as the person who owns the operational half of the agenda. When the conversation lands on "we need to hire three more people to handle this workload," they're the one asking whether the constraint is actually headcount or whether it's a broken handoff, unclear ownership, or a process that's generating work nobody needs to be doing. They make the call.

They're working directly with your team leads — the person running customer success, the head of finance, whoever's running delivery or fulfilment. They're clarifying what success looks like for each function, building the structure that lets people know what they own, and fixing the places where responsibility is unclear or work is falling through the cracks.

They're building or rebuilding the systems that let your business run without you as the bottleneck. That might be a hiring process that actually works, a project management structure that people follow because it's useful (not because you asked nicely), a budgeting cadence that surfaces real decisions instead of spreadsheet theatre, or an onboarding program that doesn't depend on you being available to answer questions in real time.

They're handling the operational decisions you used to make — often the ones you didn't realise were decisions until they landed on your desk at 4pm on a Friday. Approving a vendor contract. Deciding whether to move someone between teams. Choosing between two equally reasonable approaches to the same problem. The fractional COO takes those decisions off your plate, makes the call, and owns the outcome.

> A fractional COO takes the operational decisions off your plate, makes the call, and owns the outcome.

And they're in it for the long term. This isn't a six-week engagement that produces a strategy deck and disappears. Most fractional COO relationships run for years, because the work is ongoing — you're not solving operations once; you're running the operational side of the business as it grows and changes.

## How a fractional COO is different from a consultant, an advisor, or a fractional CEO

These terms get used interchangeably. They shouldn't.

**Fractional COO vs. consultant:** A consultant diagnoses problems, recommends solutions, and hands you a report. A fractional COO stays and executes the solution. Consultants are project-based; fractional COOs are ongoing. If someone's selling you a six-week engagement that ends with a deck, that's consulting — even if they call themselves fractional.

**Fractional COO vs. advisor:** An advisor gives you strategic input, often in specific areas — you meet monthly, they weigh in on your plans, they connect you to resources. A fractional COO is embedded in your operations, making decisions and owning execution. Advisors don't attend your weekly leadership meetings or manage your team leads. Fractional COOs do.

**Fractional COO vs. fractional CEO:** A fractional CEO owns the overall direction of the company — vision, strategy, board relations, fundraising, major partnerships. A fractional COO owns the operational execution of that direction — making sure the business runs well, the team is structured to deliver, and the systems scale. In some businesses, a fractional CEO brings in a fractional COO to handle the execution side; in others, the founder stays as CEO and hires the fractional COO to take operations off their plate. The line is: CEO sets direction, COO makes the business run.

## What does a fractional COO cost in Canada?

<div class="answer-block"><p>Fractional COO pricing is scope-dependent. It varies based on hours per week, operational complexity, and the structure of the engagement — and there's no universal rate that maps cleanly across businesses at different stages.</p></div>

What most founders want to know is whether it's affordable before they're ready to hire full-time. The honest answer: for businesses that need this kind of leadership, fractional almost always costs significantly less than a full-time hire at the same seniority level — without the benefits, equity, and full-time commitment.

> If someone's quoting you a flat rate before they've understood your business, ask what's included and what isn't. Good fractional COO work is scoped to what you actually need — not packaged at a standard tier.

If you want to understand what a fractional COO engagement would cost for your specific situation, the right place to start is a conversation. Scope determines pricing, and scope depends on where your business actually is.

## How do you know if you need a fractional COO?

<div class="answer-block"><p>You need a fractional COO if you're the operational bottleneck costing growth, your team has outgrown your systems, or you need senior operational leadership but aren't ready for a full-time hire. It's too early if you're still finding product-market fit or don't have team leads yet.</p></div>

### You need a fractional COO if:

**You're the operational bottleneck, and it's costing you growth.** Every process decision flows through you. Your team leads are waiting on you to approve things, clarify ownership, or decide between two reasonable options. You're good at operations, but you're also the CEO (or the founder, or the revenue driver), and the operational load is keeping you from doing the work only you can do.

**Your team has outgrown your systems.** What worked at 10 people doesn't work at 30. You're hiring faster than you're building structure, and new people are either inventing their own processes or waiting to be told what to do. There's no clear ownership, handoffs are breaking, and you're spending more time fixing miscommunication than running the business.

**You've tried consultants and they didn't stick.** You've paid for strategy decks that made sense on paper but never translated into action. You don't need another diagnosis; you need someone who stays and owns the execution.

**You're not ready to hire a full-time COO, but you need someone in the room.** Maybe you're at $3M in revenue and a full-time executive hire doesn't make sense yet. Maybe you're not sure what kind of COO you'd even hire. A fractional COO gets you senior operational leadership now, and gives you time to figure out what full-time looks like later — or whether you even need it.

### You don't need a fractional COO yet if:

**You're still figuring out product-market fit.** If your primary constraint is "do people want this thing," operations isn't the unlock. Get the business model working first; then hire someone to help you scale it.

**You don't have team leads to work with.** A fractional COO works through your leadership layer — if you're a five-person team where everyone reports to the founder, you probably need your first manager or two before you need a COO.

> Fractional COO only works if you're actually delegating operational ownership.

**You're not willing to let someone else make decisions.** Fractional COO only works if you're actually delegating operational ownership. If you want someone to execute your decisions but not make their own, you're looking for a project manager or an executive assistant, not a COO.

### It's too late for a fractional COO (and you need full-time) if:

**Your operations are complex enough to fill 40 hours a week.** If you're running multiple locations, managing 100+ people, or operating in a heavily regulated space, you probably need someone full-time. A fractional COO can get you there — many businesses hire fractional first, then convert to full-time once the scope justifies it — but if you're already at that scale, don't under-hire.

## Questions to ask before hiring a fractional COO

If you're talking to fractional COOs, here are the questions that will tell you whether you've found the right one:

**What does a fractional COO actually do — and what will you specifically own in an engagement with us?** You're looking for specificity here — not "I do whatever the client needs," but a clear picture of what they take off your plate. If they can't name the functions they own or the decisions they make, they're probably selling consulting, not fractional COO work.

**How is a fractional COO different from a consultant?** If they struggle to answer this — or describe a model that ends with a deliverable — that's your answer.

**How do you structure your time each week?** Are they in your leadership meetings? Working directly with your team leads? Available for ad-hoc decisions, or only during scheduled hours? You need to know what "10 hours a week" actually looks like in practice.

**What does success look like six months in?** If they can't paint a picture of what measurably changes in your business — clearer ownership, faster hiring, fewer bottlenecks, better team lead performance — they're not thinking about outcomes; they're thinking about activity.

**How do you handle accountability when something you own doesn't work?** A fractional COO who talks only about successes and never about how they've handled failure is either lying or hasn't been in the role long enough to have owned a real outcome.

**What's your approach to working with founders who are used to owning operations?** This is the transition question. A good fractional COO knows that handing off operational control is hard, and they'll have a clear plan for how that handoff happens — not just "trust me," but a real onboarding process that earns your trust week by week.

**Am I ready to actually let go?** This one's on you, not them. And a fractional COO worth hiring will ask you this question directly, because the engagement only works if you're ready to answer yes.
