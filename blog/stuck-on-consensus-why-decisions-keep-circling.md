When the same item keeps showing up on your agenda, stop and ask why. What is actually stopping you from doing something about it? Either it is a no, we are not doing this, or it is a yes, we are. Anything in between has a cost, and you are paying it every week.

## Why does a decision keep circling when the team has all the facts?

<div class="answer-block">
<p>Usually one of four things: nobody has been told the call is theirs, the room does not share the same information, a no was quietly overruled by silence, or there is a real disagreement nobody has said out loud.</p>
</div>

If you have a capable team, plenty of opinions and a decision that keeps circling, the [Stuck on Consensus page](/who-we-help/stuck-on-consensus) walks through those four. Which one is true here?

Then ask the harder question. What does it cost to keep waiting? Every time you defer, something else does not move. Are you aware of what is moving and what is not?

## When is a decision not enough?

<div class="answer-block">
<p>When nobody acts on it. A decision is one thing and acting on it is another. A yes from the people you asked does not give you a start date, or someone told to begin.</p>
</div>

I saw this with a program we had already committed to putting in place, and which was required anyway. Two of us worked out what it needed: who had to be trained, how the reporting and follow-up would work, and where it sat in the budget. We took the plan to three different groups, including the board. Every question was answered. Nothing happened.

It happened when an event needed it, and something there tested what we had. We had most of it, but not all of it. We had never rehearsed the process, so we pieced parts of it together while it was already unfolding.

The delay kept costing us. At later events we hired the training in each time. Training a few of our own people to train others would have been the cheaper investment.

## What does it cost to keep asking for input?

<div class="answer-block">
<p>More than it looks like. Asking for more input feels like progress and spends nothing, while the cost shows up later and somewhere else.</p>
</div>

In another organization we ran an engagement survey. It took months to share the results. Each team was meant to take its results and make a plan. Very little happened.

What stuck was the cheapest thing on the list: half an hour every couple of weeks for the team to get together and talk, so people knew each other better. It was good, and it built some trust. The bigger issues the survey found were mostly ignored. Fixing them meant investing in technology or training, or having open conversations about what was and was not working. Those cost real money and time.

Which fix on your list is cheap, and which is expensive? Which one did you do?

## Why does a team stop telling you the true thing?

<div class="answer-block">
<p>Because they have learned it does not land. A team that hesitates to raise a question on a low-risk decision may say nothing at all when the stakes are high.</p>
</div>

I do not think a circling decision always means people feel unsafe. But it is worth asking. If something here were failing, would anyone tell you? If shipping something could hurt someone, would they say so? If everyone is going along to get along, what have you allowed to continue, even if you did not start it?

I have seen good leaders lose their team's honesty without noticing. They became hard to reach. Standing meetings lapsed, and nobody noticed for months. If you missed a regular meeting, would anyone say so? Whose complaint do you believe: the loudest, or the one closest to the work? When something you said would happen did not, did anyone tell you?

When the front door closes, information finds another route. I kept sending the updates and I asked people I trusted near the leader to carry what mattered. That is not gossip. It is a sign that the direct route is closed.

## Who owns the next step?

<div class="answer-block">
<p>One person responsible for acting and one accountable for the call. Everyone else is consulted before it is decided, or informed after.</p>
</div>

This is where RACI matters. Responsible is who does the work. Accountable is who owns the outcome and makes the call. Consulted is who gives input before the decision. Informed is who is told after.

A stuck decision often has too many people consulted and no one responsible. Everyone has been asked, and nobody has been told to begin. Count the people who have been consulted. Then name the one person responsible for acting. If you cannot, that may be why it is stuck.

Keep the numbers right. Consult only the people whose input changes the decision. Inform everyone else, as much as they need. Put a date on it.

Then let them do the work. Ownership without authority is only a title. If you hand someone a task and then take it back, you have not given them the job. I make the same point about [how much authority to give a fractional COO](/blog/consultant-fractional-coo-or-full-time-coo-how-to-choose.html).

## What would I do first?

<div class="answer-block">
<p>Find out why it keeps coming back, say yes or no, and make it someone's job to start.</p>
</div>

1. When the same item keeps returning, ask why, and decide yes or no.
2. Know what is moving and what is not. Goals and a dashboard help, so it is not just a feeling. I wrote about [EOS Rocks and OKRs](/blog/eos-rocks-vs-okrs.html) for that.
3. Build enough trust that someone can say, "We cannot move forward unless we do this." Treat delay as a risk you account for.
4. Ask why you are not hearing the whole story.
5. Name the one person responsible, the one accountable and the date.
