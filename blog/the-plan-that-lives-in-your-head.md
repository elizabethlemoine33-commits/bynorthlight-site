You know what is in motion. Some of it is nearly done, some of it stalled two weeks ago, and one or two things are quietly dead. You are the only one who can tell which is which. I have been the person holding it together, so I know how it happens.

## What does it cost when the plan only lives in your head?

<div class="answer-block">
<p>Other people cannot act on what they cannot see. Decisions only you can make queue up, and the longer the plan stays private, the harder it is to use.</p>
</div>

I once had a plan in my head for restructuring an organization. It meant starting from a clean sheet of paper. What actually moves the needle? What are our true priorities? What jobs do we need, and which of our people might fit them? A group of us worked through it carefully. It was hard and exciting, and you could see where people would come alive in their roles.

It came months too late. Things changed, and the plan could not be put into effect. The restructuring still had to happen, and a new group took it on. They did not want my plan. I sat through meetings, stakeholder gatherings and interviews holding something that very few people had, and the people who did have it were not saying so. The person who needed to say yes did not know the plan. Trust had broken, and no one was going to tell them.

By the time it was all said and done, the restructuring came about a year later than it should have.

Here is what I took from it. Waiting did not make the decision easier. It did not make it easier if people had to be let go, and it did not help to hide that a reset was being considered. People need to hear that concerns are being weighed and that their feedback is going to the right place. Being open about that builds trust all around.

Silence like that is common. Research from Harvard Business School finds that when people judge speaking up to be [unsafe or futile](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/do-i-dare-say-something), they say less, so what a leader most needs to hear can stay unsaid.

## What should you hand over first?

<div class="answer-block">
<p>The part only you do, over and over. A recurring job that always waits for you is the first thing to teach someone else.</p>
</div>

For a long time, I was the person who got a recurring set of data ready to be imported into our CRM.

Every time, I exported the data and cleaned it up. I changed the date formats, found the IDs for leads and contacts, created the organization records and built the upload files. A regular round took anywhere from one to eight hours. A big one could take a week.

While I was doing that, I was not doing strategic work, which was part of my job. The people who owned the work did not have the time or the training. So the data went in slowly, and that held up everything that depended on it: contacting people, follow-up messages, and accurate reports at the end of a quarter.

Eventually, two people on my team were trained well enough and had the capacity. I still reviewed their work. One of them enjoyed it, and took over most of it. That gave me back time for strategy, planning, documentation, reports and supporting the rest of the team. It was a big reduction in stress.

It fits what Paul Nutt found in a [2002 study of more than 400 decisions](https://news.osu.edu/half-of-business-decisions-fail-because-of-managements-blunders-new-study-finds/). Decisions made by involving the people affected succeeded more than 80% of the time, yet that approach was used in only about one decision in five.

## How much of the plan has to go on paper?

<div class="answer-block">
<p>Enough for someone else to act on it. Not every step.</p>
</div>

On a recurring process, we once mapped every step. There were about 130 of them. It was too detailed, and once it went into a task tool it was overwhelming. People asked what was supposed to be theirs and what was someone else's.

There was pushback from the beginning.

So we grouped steps together and built standard processes. One task on the list might hide seven things for marketing or twenty for the event team, and they knew what was inside it. Twice a year, each of us checked with our people and we talked as a group about what was and was not working. Then we edited the document together. By the end it was about 35 tasks. It worked because everyone could see what was theirs.

That is the version of getting it out of your head I would aim for. Not a complete record, but a plan someone else can pick up. I wrote about [what happens when a hire does not clear the bottleneck](/blog/you-hired-them-now-what.html) if that is where you are.

## Who can you tell?

<div class="answer-block">
<p>A small group of people you trust, who will think it through with you instead of agreeing because you want to do something.</p>
</div>

Before anything else, I would tell a founder to find a group of trusted people they can talk to. Lead out of a team. A team forces conversations to happen. If they are the right people, they are not just saying yes because you said you wanted to do something. They really think it through.

Then say it out loud: we need to make this decision, and I am struggling with it.

## What would I do first?

<div class="answer-block">
<p>Sort what is in motion from what has stalled, give one person the whole picture, and give each decision an owner.</p>
</div>

1. Write down what is in motion, what has stalled and what is quietly dead.
2. Hand the whole picture to one person you trust.
3. Pick the recurring job that always waits for you, and teach it to someone.
4. Give each decision an owner, so someone other than you knows what the next call is.
5. Agree what "done" looks like, so nobody has to argue about it later.

Bain's work on [decision roles](https://www.bain.com/insights/who-has-the-d/) points the same way: one person holds final authority and commits the organization to act, and too many people giving input slows decisions down.
