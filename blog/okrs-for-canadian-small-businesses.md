You have a strategy. You have ambitions. You might even have a three-year plan written somewhere on a slide deck that hasn't been opened since last quarter. And yet somehow, every 90 days ends with your to-do list longer than it started, your team stretched across a dozen initiatives, and a quiet, persistent sense that none of it is quite moving you forward fast enough.

You're not struggling because you're doing the wrong things. You're struggling because you're doing too many things without a clear filter for what actually moves the business toward its vision.

That's the problem OKRs were designed to solve.

## What OKRs Actually Are

OKR stands for Objectives and Key Results. The framework was developed at Intel in the 1970s by CEO Andy Grove, popularised at Google in its earliest years, and brought to a wider business audience through John Doerr's 2018 book [*Measure What Matters*](https://www.whatmatters.com/).

The structure is simple:

- An **Objective** is a qualitative statement of where you want to go — inspiring, directional, time-bound
- **Key Results** are specific, measurable outcomes that tell you whether you've actually arrived

An Objective without Key Results is just aspiration. Key Results without an Objective are just metrics. Together, they answer two questions every business needs to answer every quarter: *where are we going, and how will we know we've gotten there?*

OKRs are not a task list. They are not a performance review tool. They are not a way to justify bonuses. They are a communication and focus mechanism — a way for an organisation to say, clearly and collectively, *these are the things that matter most right now.*

## Why This Matters More Right Now

If you're running a Canadian business in 2026, you're operating in an environment where the headline and the reality don't always match. Small business confidence reached a three-year high in Q2 2026. GDP grew at its strongest pace in five quarters. And in the same quarter, exports fell sharply on new tariff uncertainty, and the picture sector by sector looked nothing like the index.

The environment can shift faster than your planning cycle. In that kind of variability, the cost of misaligned priorities isn't just inefficiency — it's time and money spent on things that don't move you forward. Choosing what to focus on has always mattered. Right now, it matters more.

## The Five Reasons OKRs Work When They Work

John Doerr describes OKR's core benefits as five "superpowers," captured in the acronym F.A.C.T.S.:

1. **Focus.** OKRs force disciplined thinking about what counts. Doerr writes in [*Measure What Matters*](https://www.whatmatters.com/): "An effective goal-setting system starts with disciplined thinking at the top, with leaders who invest the time and energy to choose what counts." When leaders focus, that clarity moves through the organisation — people stop second-guessing whether they're working on the right things.

2. **Alignment.** OKRs make it possible for every person in the organisation to understand how their work connects to the organisation's top-line goals. Misalignment creates silos, internal competition, and stagnation. Aligned teams move together.

3. **Commitment.** People commit to things they understand. OKRs make the organisation's priorities visible and legible — which makes it possible to ask people to genuinely commit rather than simply comply.

4. **Tracking.** OKRs are an active process, not a passive one. They require regular check-ins and honest progress reporting. Doerr quotes Peter Drucker: "Without an action plan, the executive becomes a prisoner of events. And without check-ins to reexamine the plan as events unfold, the executive has no way of knowing which events really matter and which are only noise."

5. **Stretching.** OKRs — specifically aspirational OKRs — are designed to push you further than you'd otherwise go. The goal isn't to set a bar you'll definitely clear. It's to set a bar that changes what you think is possible.

## The Three Kinds of OKRs — And Which One to Use

Not all OKRs carry the same expectations. There are three types, and mixing them up is one of the most common sources of frustration:

**Committed OKRs** are non-negotiable. You must achieve 100% by the end of the cycle. These represent work essential to the organisation's health — keeping the lights on, meeting a contractual obligation, hitting a financial floor. Think of them as the things that must happen.

**Aspirational OKRs** set a higher bar by design. Many organisations, including Google, consider 70% achievement a success on aspirational OKRs — because the goal exists to push you further, not to set a ceiling you're certain to clear. If you're consistently hitting 100% on aspirational OKRs, you're not setting them ambitiously enough.

**Learning OKRs** are used when you need to gather information before you can set meaningful committed or aspirational goals. If you're entering a new market, testing a new offer, or trying to understand why something isn't working, a Learning OKR defines what you want to know and how you'll know when you know it.

The critical rule: **decide which type each OKR is before the cycle begins.** If your team thinks a goal is aspirational but you're treating it as committed, they'll assume underperformance is expected. If they think it's committed but you're running it as aspirational, they'll be demoralised when they fall short of 100%.

## How to Write Key Results That Actually Work

Key Results are where most OKR implementations break down. The most common mistake is writing Key Results that are really just tasks — activities to check off rather than outcomes to achieve.

A strong Key Result passes three tests:

1. **It is specific and time-bound.** "Improve our customer experience" is not a Key Result. "Reduce average client response time from 48 hours to 24 hours by end of Q3" is a Key Result.

2. **It is aggressive yet realistic.** A Key Result that's too easy produces no meaningful information. A Key Result that's impossible produces demoralisation. The goal is ambitious enough to require real effort, achievable enough to be worth pursuing.

3. **It is measurable and verifiable.** At the end of the cycle, there should be no debate about whether the Key Result was met. Anyone on the team should be able to look at the number and answer the question: did we get there or not?

The other test worth applying: are your Key Results measuring *outcomes* or *outputs*? "Launch three client workshops" is an output. "Increase client-reported confidence scores by 20 points following workshop participation" is an outcome. Outputs tell you what you did. Outcomes tell you whether it worked.

## Why OKR Implementations Fail

[Research from the OKR Institute](https://okrinstitute.org/) estimates that 60–70% of OKR implementations fail. Some sources put the abandonment rate at 70% within two years. In advisory practice, the patterns are consistent and predictable.

The most common failure modes, in order:

1. **Perfect becomes the enemy of good.** Teams spend so much time trying to write perfect OKRs before the first cycle that they never actually run one. Start with fewer, simpler OKRs. The learning happens in the doing, not in the drafting. *Signal: if drafting takes more than two working sessions, start the cycle anyway.*

2. **OKR work gets crowded out.** Business as usual expands to fill available time. It's easy to spend a full quarter attending to urgent, familiar work and then arrive at the end of the cycle having made no progress on the things that were supposed to matter most. Weekly check-ins exist specifically to prevent this. *Fix: skip two weekly check-ins in a row, and the OKRs are effectively cancelled.*

3. **Too many OKRs.** If everything is a priority, nothing is. Too many OKRs usually means they're too granular — you've loaded your framework with tasks instead of focused outcomes. Ask: have we narrowed down to the things that would genuinely change the business? *Signal: if you can't recite all your Key Results from memory, you probably have too many.*

4. **OKRs are tied to compensation.** This is reliably the fastest way to destroy an OKR practice. As recounted in [*Measure What Matters*](https://www.whatmatters.com/), Laszlo Bock — then Senior VP of People Operations at Google — described what happened the one year Google connected OKR performance directly to pay: "People started gaming the system to get their bonuses." When stretch goals carry financial risk, people stop stretching. *Fix: keep OKRs completely separate from performance reviews and bonus discussions — in both design and conversation.*

5. **Key Results look like to-do lists.** If your Key Results read like a project plan — "develop content strategy," "hire marketing coordinator," "review vendor contracts" — you've built a task list, not an OKR. Key Results describe results, not activity. *Signal: if you can check a Key Result off without measuring anything, it's a task.*

6. **The strategic link is missing.** OKRs that don't connect clearly to organisational strategy become a parallel process that competes with — rather than reinforces — existing priorities. Teams start asking why they're doing two planning systems instead of one. *Signal: if your team can't answer "why does this OKR matter to the company?" in one sentence, the strategic link is missing.*

7. **There is no parking lot.** One of the most underrated failure modes: no mechanism for deferring good ideas that aren't current priorities. Without a deliberate holding area for ideas that are real but not right now, organisations accumulate active commitments until everything is theoretically in progress and nothing is actually advancing. *Fix: before the OKR session ends, ask what good ideas didn't make the list. Write them down somewhere the team can see them.*

We have seen each of these failure modes play out in real organisations. The seventh is the quietest and the most insidious — an organisation can spend an entire quarter in motion without ever asking whether the motion is toward anything that matters.

## What Actually Makes OKRs Work

The research and the experience point to the same conclusion: the conditions in which you introduce OKRs matter more than the OKRs themselves.

A methodology can perform badly in an environment that isn't ready for it. OKRs require:

- **Strategic clarity.** OKRs translate strategy into action. They can't create the strategy. If there is no shared answer to "where are we going and why," OKRs will surface that gap rather than paper over it.
- **Organisational trust.** Honest reporting — including honest reporting of failure — requires psychological safety. If people fear consequences for saying a priority didn't progress, the data you get back will be managed rather than accurate.
- **Clear ownership.** Every OKR needs a named owner. Not a committee. Not "the team." One person who is accountable for progress and can give a concise update at any check-in.
- **Willingness to say no.** As Andy Grove wrote in [*High Output Management*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Output_Management): "A few extremely well-chosen Objectives impart a clear message about what we say 'yes' to and what we say 'no' to." If your organisation can't say no to new requests, OKRs will become one more thing on a list that has no end.

In practice, the gap most often shows up before the OKR session even begins. Teams arrive without shared answers to where they're going and why — and attempt to write Key Results for an Objective that hasn't been genuinely agreed upon. The OKRs surface the disagreement, but they can't resolve it. The work that makes OKRs function is usually the work that has to happen before them.

## Getting Started: A Practical Sequence

If you're ready to try OKRs in your business, here is a practical starting sequence:

1. **Start at the top.** Identify no more than three Objectives for the organisation for the coming quarter. These should reflect your most important strategic priorities — not everything important, just the things that most need to move.

2. **Write Key Results before you finalise Objectives.** Work backwards: if you achieve this Objective, what would you be able to measure? Let that answer shape both the Objective and the KRs.

3. **Designate each OKR as committed or aspirational before the cycle begins.** Make this explicit and visible to everyone involved.

4. **Assign one owner to each OKR.** That person is responsible for tracking progress and flagging blockers. Others may contribute, but ownership is not shared.

5. **Build a weekly check-in into your rhythm.** This does not have to be long — 15 minutes is enough to ask: where are we on each Key Result, what's in the way, and what do we need to do differently?

6. **Grade honestly at the end of the cycle.** Score each Key Result on its own terms, then reflect on what the scores tell you. A missed commitment is a conversation about what went wrong. A strong aspirational score might mean you set the bar too low.

Your first cycle will be imperfect. That's expected. The learning from cycle one informs cycle two, and OKRs tend to get meaningfully better by the third or fourth cycle as teams develop shared language and sharper instincts for what good Key Results actually look like.

## Three OKRs in Practice: A Northlight Example

The clearest way to make this concrete is to see all three types side by side. Here is what a quarter of OKRs looks like for a practice like Northlight — one committed, one aspirational, one learning.

**Committed OKR** *(must reach 100%)*

*Objective: Deliver reliably on every active client engagement this quarter.*

1. Complete all agreed deliverables for current client engagements by contracted deadlines — 100%.
2. Conduct a structured mid-quarter check-in with each active client.
3. Invoice within five business days of each deliverable milestone.

Grading expectation: 100%. Anything less is a conversation about what went wrong.

---

**Aspirational OKR** *(70% is a win)*

*Objective: Make bynorthlight.ca a go-to resource for Canadian founders navigating strategy and operations.*

1. Publish six long-form posts that each earn at least one backlink from an external source.
2. Grow organic search traffic by 40% quarter-over-quarter.
3. Receive 10 direct referrals to bynorthlight.ca from other Canadian business resources.

Grading expectation: 70% or above is a success. If we hit 100%, we set the bar too low.

---

**Learning OKR** *(outcome is a decision, not a score)*

*Objective: Determine whether a packaged AI governance workshop is worth developing for small business clients.*

1. Pilot the concept with two clients by end of Q3, with structured feedback collected from each.
2. Document findings on pricing, format, duration, and buyer motivation.
3. Make a go / no-go decision on formal development by the end of the quarter.

Grading expectation: Success means we have a clear answer — yes, no, or not yet and why. The outcome is the decision itself.

---

What these illustrate: the committed OKR has no flexibility built in, the aspirational one sets a bar we'd be proud to hit 70% of, and the learning one is judged entirely by whether we made a decision, not by how any numbers landed. Different expectations, different grading, same discipline.
