Here's something worth sitting with: LinkedIn ranks second in the world for AI citations, behind only YouTube; it generates eleven times more AI citations than Quora. That data comes from Meltwater's [analysis of 9 million AI citations](https://www.linkedin.com/business/marketing/blog/ai-search){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}, published in 2026.

That's the headline. The detail inside it is what's actually useful: **75% of LinkedIn's AI citations come from individual profiles, not company pages.**

Which means if you've been building your content strategy around your company page — posting from it, putting your thought leadership there, treating your personal profile as the secondary presence — you've been putting your energy in the wrong place. Not for engagement reasons; for citation reasons. For the moment when a potential client asks an AI tool to tell them about your field, and the model is deciding whose voice to quote.

## Why personal profiles outperform company pages

This isn't arbitrary; it reflects how AI models are trained and what they reward.

Company pages tend to post polished, branded content — announcements, curated articles, event promotions. That content is readable, but it reads like marketing; and AI models, trained on enormous amounts of human-written text, have gotten reasonably good at identifying content that exists to sell something rather than to inform.

Individual profiles tell a different story. When a person writes about what they've learned, what they've observed in their clients' organisations, what surprised them about a project — that's experience-based content. It has a point of view; it makes claims and backs them up. That's exactly what a model looks for when it's constructing an answer: a credible source saying something specific.

The Meltwater research also found that structured articles with clear headings get cited more often than unstructured posts — and this tracks with the broader GEO research. [Aggarwal et al. (2024)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.09735){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} found that clearly organized, well-structured content raises AI visibility by up to 40% across platforms generally. LinkedIn articles, with their indexable titles and heading structure, fit that profile exactly. A LinkedIn article with defined sections and a clear argument is more AI-citable than a series of line-break posts, even if those posts get significantly more engagement in the feed.

## What company pages are actually good for

Before this turns into "delete your company page" — that's not the point. Company pages have legitimate uses: they show up in branded searches, they provide a verification signal, they're where employees list their employer. Those things matter.

What company pages are not good for is building the kind of credibility that AI models draw from. The 25% of LinkedIn citations that do come from company pages tend to be from organisations that have built an extensive, consistent body of original content over time — the kind of sustained publishing cadence that most SMBs can't maintain at the company level, and probably shouldn't try to.

The better investment, by a significant margin, is in the personal profile of the person whose expertise is the actual product.

## What this means for your content strategy

A few practical shifts worth making:

**Move your thought leadership to your personal profile.** Your company page can stay active for announcements and reposts — but the writing that positions you as a practitioner in your field should come from you, not from it.

**Write LinkedIn articles, not just feed posts.** LinkedIn articles — the long-form format accessible from your profile — are indexed differently than feed posts. They have titles and headings; they're crawlable; they persist in a way that a post from three weeks ago doesn't. Write the pieces worth writing as articles, and treat short posts as teasers that point back to them.

**Use structure deliberately.** Clear headings, a stated point of view, and something specific — a number, a framework, an observation from your own work — are what make a piece of content AI-citable. "Here's what I've learned" without a claim is a story. "Here's what I've learned, and here's what it means for businesses in this situation" is a citation.

**Be consistent on a topic.** AI models build an understanding of an entity — you, in this case — from repeated signals. Publishing regularly on a specific subject builds a clearer identity than posting randomly across ten different ones. Decide what you want to be known for, and write toward it.

**Get your personal profile into indexed professional directories.** LinkedIn is the most powerful, but it's not the only platform that creates personal citation signals. Directories with individual profile pages — like [Digital Reference's founder directory](https://www.digitalreference.co/directory/founder){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} — are indexed and crawled, which means being consistently listed there with your name, expertise, and credentials contributes to the same entity recognition you're building on LinkedIn. Some platforms also offer opportunities to contribute articles or be featured in category roundups; Digital Reference does (the directory profile itself is the starting point).

The businesses I see building AI citation footprints intentionally are not doing anything exotic. They're writing clearly, publishing consistently, and putting that content where the research says it gets picked up. That's a repeatable practice, not a technical project — which means it's available to anyone who decides to make it a priority.

The alternative — putting everything through your company page and hoping — isn't a strategy. It's a habit.
