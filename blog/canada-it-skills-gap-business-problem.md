91% of Canadian organizations report IT human resources challenges. That number is up from 78% just a few years ago, according to NOVIPRO's 2026 IT Trends Report ([p.33](https://www.novipro.com/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}). And 45% cite training and skills development as their top concern.

This is usually filed under HR's problem. It isn't.

The IT skills gap is a business continuity problem — one that's compounding quietly, in the background, at exactly the moment when Canadian businesses are adding more AI tools to their operations and becoming more dependent on technical infrastructure to do basic work.

## What the numbers actually say

The [ICTC Digital Talent Outlook 2025](https://ictc-ctic.ca/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} projected 2.26 million digital economy jobs in Canada against a 3.3% unemployment rate in the sector — with an estimated 250,000 positions that couldn't be filled. CompTIA's data puts approximately 1.5 million tech workers in Canada total. The gap isn't evenly distributed: it's most acute in AI, cloud, and cybersecurity — exactly the areas where adoption is accelerating.

NOVIPRO found that 79% of Canadian organizations use external IT firms to supplement their internal teams. That's a significant majority of Canadian businesses already operating in a model where IT capability is outsourced, which means it's subject to the same pressures, delays, and availability constraints as any other external vendor relationship.

For a small business, this means: the IT partner you depend on is managing the same talent shortage you are. When they're stretched, your support slows down. When they lose a key person, that expertise disappears. When the market for IT talent gets tighter — which, by every available indicator, it is — the cost and availability of that support becomes an active business constraint.

## The AI dependency that nobody planned for

Microsoft's Work Trend Index 2025, cited in NOVIPRO's 2026 IT Trends Report ([p.32](https://www.novipro.com/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}), found that 75% of employees are using AI tools at work without formal guidance from their organizations.

That number should give you pause if you're a business owner who has been thinking of AI adoption as an IT decision. Seventy-five per cent of your team is making AI decisions right now, without a framework, without oversight, and in many cases without anyone in the business having the background to evaluate whether what they're doing is appropriate.

The IT skills gap makes this worse, not better. If the gap means you don't have someone on your team — or readily accessible through your IT partner — who can evaluate whether a new AI tool is safe, how it handles your data, or whether it integrates appropriately with your other systems, then the default is to adopt first and find out later. That's the pattern NOVIPRO's data reflects: high adoption rates, low governance rates, and a widening gap between the two.

This is not a technology problem. It's a business operations problem.

## What 79% external dependency actually means

The 79% figure — nearly four in five Canadian organizations supplementing their IT with external firms — is worth taking seriously as an operational dependency.

Most small businesses don't manage their IT partner relationship with the same rigour they'd apply to a key supplier or a critical contractor. There's no SLA review. There's no escalation path documented. There's no backup plan if the partner becomes unavailable. And unlike a supplier of physical goods, where you can find an alternative in days, an IT partner with knowledge of your systems, your tools, and your configurations takes months to replace effectively.

If AI tools are now integrated into how your team does its daily work — and for most businesses that have adopted them, they are — then your IT dependency has become a core operational dependency. It should be managed like one.

## What businesses are doing well

The organisations I see navigating this well are not the ones with the most sophisticated IT teams. They're the ones that have treated IT decisions as business decisions.

Specifically, they've done three things:

**Built internal capability for the decisions they make repeatedly.** You don't need an in-house developer to evaluate whether a new AI tool is appropriate for your business. You do need someone — internal or fractional — who understands the questions to ask: what data does it access, what are the terms, who's responsible if something goes wrong. That capability can be built without a full IT hire.

**Managed their external IT relationships as vendor relationships.** Defined scope, defined escalation, documented configurations, a backup plan. The same rigour you'd apply to any other key supplier.

**Used AI tools strategically to extend capacity without requiring specialist staff.** The irony of the IT skills gap is that the tools designed to help close it — AI tools that automate routine tasks, surface patterns in data, draft documentation — are often adopted without the governance structure that makes them safe to use. The businesses using AI effectively are the ones who went in with a plan, even a simple one. (If you haven't built that plan yet, [the post on AI strategy](/blog/canadian-businesses-ai-adoption-strategy.html) is a reasonable place to start.)

The IT skills gap is real, it's worsening, and it's not going to be solved by the national talent pipeline in time to help your business in the next 12 months. What you can control is how you manage the dependency you already have — and whether you're building the internal decision-making capability that keeps your operations resilient regardless of what's happening in the talent market.
